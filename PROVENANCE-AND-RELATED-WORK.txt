Sources, formalization and related work

The selected development combines local classification of labeled frame triples, generation and quantitative estimates, recognition from an exact finite word table, recognition from a supplied tangent metric and full-curvature match, and the full derivative of Phi. The 23 statements are listed individually in THEOREMS.md. Their source is a collection of retained arguments and unpublished notes on compact Albert reconstruction and finite observations, together with the classical ingredients and software reuse identified below. No wholesale formalization of those notes, exhaustive priority assessment, or novelty claim is made. All project exposition and Lean code are AI-generated under Robert MacGregor's direction; the source reviews are AI reviews, not independent human mathematical review.

Classical mathematics used in the development

The model H_3(O), its half Jordan product, raw trace and positive trace pairing are classical. Baez's exposition gives the compact model and its reference automorphism properties [B, §§3.4 and 4.2; arXiv v4 pp.31–32 and 39–40]. The Lean development constructs the needed native algebra, diagonalization and transport maps. The selected statements use explicit product-and-trace automorphisms; they do not select a proof identifying their full abstract group with F4. Baez is credited as an expository source, not as the originator of the Albert algebra.

Glennie's degree-eight special identity is the classical antecedent of the vector polynomial used here [G, §2, relation (8), p.56, and degree-eight relation (9), p.57]. The adopted formula is McCrimmon's introductory convention: the unhalved triple product and final input x circle y [M, §0.1, pp.5–6]. His Appendix B convention uses the two-tad in that slot and differs by a factor of two [M, Definition B.5.2, p.469]. In the package, glennieVector has the introductory normalization. Selected statement 22 proves that the actual full derivative of Phi is the sum of the three cyclic raw-trace pairings with this vector. This credits Glennie's polynomial and McCrimmon's normalization without attributing the project's derivative identity, local compact-real orbit theorem, or generation criterion to those sources.

A concrete supporting classical identity is U_p(q)=B(p,q)p for a trace-one idempotent p. Garibaldi–Petersson–Racine establish the corresponding elementary-idempotent identity in the proof of Proposition 37.2(b); §37.1 supplies their elementary-idempotent definition and Proposition 37.4 treats orthogonal frames [GPR, January 19, 2026 draft, printed pp.362–365]. The native file TraceOneU.lean explicitly credits these passages as proof guidance and derives the identity from the native cubic residual. It assumes trace one and idempotence, not an extra unproved sharp-map condition. Their circle convention and the package's half Jordan product must be translated. This is a supporting identity, not an additional selected headline or a formalization of the entire book.

Software reuse is a separate attribution. Native/CubicJordan.lean adapts the general cubic-characteristic-identity implication from the earlier public split-Albert project at commit dd06ef5d423a0e4a5d814d2c221e999b98887ee1 [SW]. It changes rational to real scalars and the module interface. The native compact cubic identity is proved separately; no split invariant-field theorem is transferred by this reuse. Lean, Mathlib and the locked dependencies retain their own credits and licenses.

Classification and cubic reconstruction: related classical results

Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner classified finite-dimensional formally real Jordan algebras. McCrimmon states the classification at [M, §1.11, p.49], citing their 1934 paper [JvNW]. It supports a conceptual explanation of why a proper compact Albert subalgebra cannot exhibit the relevant exceptional behavior. The inspected Lean generation proof instead uses actual simultaneous pair reduction, the reduced Phi formula and coordinate-span arguments in NativeGenerationEndpointR01.lean. It does not invoke that classification as a proof shortcut. The original 1934 paper is credited historically; the checked statement here is McCrimmon's account, not a new audit of the original proof.

Shirshov–Cohn says that a Jordan algebra generated by two elements is special; see [M, Theorem II.5.1.3, p.200, and Theorem B.4.3, p.468]. This is relevant background to generator counts. It does not by itself place an arbitrary compact Albert pair in the particular native complex sector required by the formal proof. The package proves that reduction explicitly. Neither a general Shirshov–Cohn theorem nor a minimum-generator theorem is one of the 23 selected statements.

Springer's construction reconstructs a Jordan product from a suitable based cubic. The explicit assumptions include a nondegenerate trace form and the adjoint identity; see Tkachev [T, Definition 2.1 and Proposition 2.1, pp.5–6], following Springer [S] and McCrimmon [M, II.4.2–4.3]. These are classical antecedents of cubic-to-product reconstruction. The package proves its needed identities and transport maps for the actual normalized cubic and compact model. The source correspondence does not justify calling the selected curvature-recognition theorem a formalization of Springer, McCrimmon or Tkachev: their cited hypotheses and conclusions are different. Springer is cited through this explicit source chain; his original article was not independently reread for this revision.

Iltyakov and the scope of the trace invariants

Iltyakov studies polynomial invariants of three Albert elements over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero [I]. Proposition 2 and relation (16), pp.9–10, give a quadratic relation of the form omega^2=p+q omega over the primary algebra. Corollary 3 and Theorem 2, pp.17–18, give the rank-two module presentation. The closing remark on p.18 treats trace-zero inputs after removing the three linear traces. This is a substantial classical antecedent for the role of an additional invariant. It is distinct from a count of complete compact-real frame orbits over each datum in an open real neighborhood. No selected statement asserts the entire Iltyakov invariant-ring presentation, identifies Phi with his omega, or turns the quadratic relation into a global compact-real orbit theorem. The citation uses the preserved primary-text extraction; original PostScript page-image confirmation remains an explicitly recorded limitation.

Cubic geometry and the curvature-recognition theorem

The forward curvature relation between a cubic and commutators of its raised multiplication operators has classical antecedents. Amari–Armstrong give the Hessian-curvature identity [AA, Proposition 1, p.4, equation (1)]. Lindemann gives the unit-level projective-special-real formula [L, Lemma 3.11, p.21, equations (3.35)–(3.37)]. The retained source comparison translates the scale and sign conventions; the level-set formula has a fixed metric-wedge term in addition to the cubic commutator. In the package, LeviCivitaCurvature.chartCurvature_zero proves the actual geometric formula, rather than assuming that desired formula as the definition of curvature.

The inverse step has a different burden. The Lean proof passes through the native reference Jacobi-kernel line and the unrestricted quadratic-curvature fiber, then reconstructs the full normalized cubic using its unit/tangent decomposition. RecognitionFromCurvature.lean consumes the supplied tangent metric and full vector-valued curvature equivalence and constructs a whole-space norm/unit equivalence, correcting a possible tangent sign. The resulting ambient equivalence need not extend the initially supplied tangent map. This argument is drawn from unpublished notes on Albert curvature recognition and proved in the package; no classical paper listed here is being credited with this exact one-point inverse theorem solely because it concerns Jordan geometry.

Related geometric recognition theorems must retain their hypotheses. Hildebrand relates Hessian potentials with parallel third derivative to metrized Jordan algebras [Hi, Lemma 3.3 and Theorem 3.9]; further first-derivative parallelism and convexity enter his symmetric-cone result [Hi, Theorem 5.8]. Li–Zhao's exceptional affine-symmetric case restricts to invariant Fubini–Pick forms [LZ, Definition 2.2, equation (4.1), Lemma 4.1 and Proposition 4.2]. Naitoh's Jordan construction uses parallel-submanifold hypotheses and the resulting invariance [N, Part II, Lemma 6.1 and equation (6.7), p.121; Theorem 6.3, p.123]. These are useful comparisons, not imported proofs that an unrestricted positive cubic is recognized by the single full-curvature match in selected statement 21. Their global or neighborhood-wide hypotheses must not be silently replaced by pointwise ones.

Exact finite-word recognition and boundaries

The selected Albert word-table theorem uses its fixed 27 reference words and three exact scalar-data families: Gram entries, product-word pairings and full ambient product squared norms. The last family rules out components perpendicular to the word span. Its unique marked embedding is proved from that finite table.

Broader symbolic work remains separately attributed. General invariant-ring identifications, global realized-primary fibers, bundle/readout topology, and a converse to the derivative-generation implication are not among the 23 selected statements. The selected quotient theorem is local, and the selected derivative-generation theorem is a forward implication. Work on homogeneous cones, parallel cubic forms or physical interpretations is not evidence that these broader claims were formalized. The bibliography records relevant mathematics, not a list of theorem dependencies or a priority claim.
